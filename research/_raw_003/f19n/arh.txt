 Flight 19: The Leader Thought He Was Over the Gulf. He Was Over the Atlantic.

 The chart of Navigation Problem No. 1, entered as the first exhibit of the board that investigated the loss, shows three legs over the sea from Fort Lauderdale and home. On the afternoon of 5 December 1945 five Navy torpedo bombers flew it as Flight 19, and none of the fourteen men came back. The video argues that the Navy’s own record never described a mystery. This page holds the sources for that video, what the record settles, and what it doesn’t.

What the video argues
Flight 19 is usually described as a mystery, and the Navy’s own record doesn’t describe one. Its board of investigation found where the flight was and why it was lost, and the Navy defended that finding against the flight leader’s family for more than half a year. The word unknown entered the story only when the finding was struck. Every time below is local Florida time, as the board kept it.
The flight said where it thought it was, and the rescue unit worked out where it really was. The flight left at about 1410 under Lieutenant Charles Carroll Taylor, the instructor in charge, flying as FT-28. At 1621 the Air Sea Rescue unit at Port Everglades copied him: “Am lost. At present time I am over small islands believed to be the Florida Keys.” Its operations officer said at 1639 that the flight was lost “somewhere over the Bahama Bank,” the other side of the Florida Straits. Taylor reported at 1645 a heading of 030 for forty-five minutes, then north, “to make sure we are not over the Gulf of Mexico.” Direction finders fixed him for 1750 within a hundred miles of 29 degrees 15 minutes north, 79 degrees west, at sea east of northern Florida, and the board called it a highly probable location.
The board named the cause and, beside it, the rescue system’s failures. Its seventeenth opinion finds that the leader “was uncertain as to in what direction lay the peninsula of Florida and this uncertainty influenced his later decisions,” and its forty-fourth that the disappearance “was caused by a temporary mental confusion resulting in faulty judgment on the part of the flight leader.” The same board found that no aircraft shifted to the Air Sea Rescue frequency, that Cuban broadcasting stations swamped the training frequency, that a rescue teletype was out of commission, and that nobody broadcast the 1750 fix to the flight.
While Congress was asking questions, the record went up the chain unchecked. On 28 December 1945 the Chief of Naval Operations asked for complete information “in order that appropriate reply may be made to Congressional inquiries.” On 21 January 1946 the command’s legal officer wrote that checking every finding “would entail days of careful analytical reading, which obviously we have not attempted to do because of the urgency in forwarding the record.” Three days later Rear Admiral Ralph Davison approved the proceedings.
When the leader’s mother objected, the Navy stood on its finding. Mrs. Taylor wrote on 22 June 1946 that “the only mention of blame made in this Record is that which is placed on my son.” An informal board of five officers met for three days to answer her, and its report of 19 July recommended a firm letter: “if the case were reopened there is every indication that Lt. Taylors responsibility for the safety of the flight might be more firmly established and stated.”
The board did write unknown in 1945, for another aircraft. Training 49, a PBM Mariner, left Banana River at about 1927 with thirteen men to search for the five bombers. At 1950 the merchant ship Gaines Mills reported a burst of flames a hundred feet high, and the escort carrier Solomons lost a plane from her radar at that moment and in that spot. The board found that the Mariner “exploded in the sea for reason or reasons unknown.” For the five Avengers it wrote no such sentence. According to the Naval History and Heritage Command, Mrs. Taylor later took her case to the Board for Correction of Naval Records, which found an injustice in her son’s record, and the cause of the loss was recorded as unknown.
What the 1945 record shows
In 1945 the Navy knew where Flight 19 was lost and why, and its own record shows that the unknown came afterward. The mystery of Flight 19 is younger than its record. The pages from 1945 still put the flight at sea east of Florida, under a leader who thought he might be over the Gulf of Mexico.

What the record does not settle
When Captain Powers took the lead, and where the flight went after 1807. The board found that Captain Edward Powers of the Marine Corps took over at the front of the formation and held 270, due west, but it could not fix the time, and it wrote that the courses flown after 1807 were undetermined.
Where the five aircraft came down. The 1750 fix is a circle of a hundred miles, which the board called a highly probable location. It found that they came down in darkness at sea, east of the peninsula, sometime after 1904, and the searches of 6 to 10 December found no wreckage and no survivors.
What destroyed Training 49. The board wrote that the Mariner “exploded in the sea for reason or reasons unknown.”
The correction board’s decision. It was not found in the National Archives’ online catalog and was not examined for this video, so what it says is given as the Naval History and Heritage Command reports it.
A note on names and times
Times are local Florida time, which the board’s record marks R. Lieutenant Charles Carroll Taylor led the flight as FT-28, and Lieutenant Robert Cox flew FT-74. The board sat at NAS Miami under the Naval Air Advanced Training Command, whose chief, Rear Admiral Ralph Davison, approved its proceedings. Training 49 is the board’s name for the PBM-5 Mariner lost in the search. The aircraft of Flight 19 were TBM Avengers.
