# How the Disappearance of Flight 19, a Navy Squadron Lost in 1945, Fueled the Legend of the Bermuda Triangle

Eighty years ago, five planes vanished during a training run off the Florida coast. A patrol plane sent to search for the men went missing, too, giving rise to a host of conspiracy theories

“I don’t know where we are,” a voice on the radio said. “We must have got lost after that last turn.”

This message was the first inkling that something was amiss with [Flight 19](https://www.nasflmuseum.com/flight-19.html), a routine United States Navy training run off the coast of Florida on December 5, 1945. In the confusing hours that followed, the five torpedo bombers’ radio transmissions grew fainter as their fuel supply dwindled. Soon, the men—a mix of Marines and naval aviators—were no longer able to communicate with land, and two naval patrol bombers were dispatched to search for them. A short time later, one of the rescue planes abruptly dropped off radio contact and disappeared, too.

In total, six aircraft carrying 27 men (14 from Flight 19 and 13 from the patrol bomber) vanished over the so-called [Bermuda Triangle](https://www.britannica.com/place/Bermuda-Triangle) that day. No confirmed trace of them has ever [been found](https://www.today.com/news/history-channel-doc-reveals-key-discovery-bermuda-triangle-t229521). Eighty years later, Flight 19 remains one of aviation’s most [notable](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/recovering-lost-aviators-world-war-ii-180983690/) [mysteries](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/the-enduring-mystery-of-a-plane-that-vanished-in-the-icy-canadian-wilderness-with-44-people-on-board-180985878/)—and where answers are elusive, legions of theories have emerged to fill in [the blanks](https://www.history.com/articles/the-mysterious-disappearance-of-flight-19).

From alien abductions to the lost continent of [Atlantis](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/the-mesoamerican-influences-behind-namor-from-black-panther-wakanda-forever-180981106/), magnetic anomalies, methane eruptions and time travel, highly improbable explanations for the disappearances of planes and ships lost in the [Bermuda Triangle](https://www.sciencefocus.com/science/bermuda-triangle) have gripped the public’s imagination. Flight 19 has even appeared in both a [Steven Spielberg](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/the-making-of-steven-spielberg-180981184/) [film](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt0075860/?ref_=nv_sr_srsg_0_tt_8_nm_0_in_0_q_close%2520encount) and a [Scooby-Doo mystery](https://scoobydoo.fandom.com/wiki/Scooby-Doo!_Pirates_Ahoy!).

Yet the real postwar tragedy that inspired these fictionalizations is a mystery that denies the imagination a satisfying explanation, supernatural or otherwise. Instead, false assumptions, fleeting communications and a hastily scrambled rescue search left behind clues as to what transpired in the final moments of Flight 19—and exposed a series of decisions that, if they’d unfolded differently, might have brought the men home safely.

## A minute-by-minute breakdown of the disappearance of Flight 19

At 2:10 p.m. on December 5, flight leader [Charles Carroll Taylor](https://www.nasflmuseum.com/taylor.html), a 28-year-old lieutenant known as “C.C.,” took off from Naval Air Station (NAS) Fort Lauderdale. He was accompanied by four other planes. The squadron’s [flight plan](https://prologue.blogs.archives.gov/2017/10/16/uncertain-as-to-in-what-position-lay-the-peninsula-of-florida-the-official-record-and-the-loss-of-flight-19/) advised the men to [practice bombing](https://www.historynet.com/five-tbm-avenger-bombers-lost-in-the-bermuda-triangle/) on a small grouping of rocks near the Bahamian island of Bimini, a distance of 56 miles, then fly up over the northern islands of the Bahamas before turning back east to Fort Lauderdale—a routine navigation exercise intended to last around 2 hours and 40 minutes.

![How the Disappearance of Flight 19, a Navy Squadron Lost in 1945, Fueled the Legend of the Bermuda Triangle](https://tf-cmsv2-smithsonianmag-media.s3.amazonaws.com/filer_public/ef/da/efda7d10-94f6-4f95-8014-45d80f99f0e7/flight_19_map.png)

A map of Flight 19's planned route and navigational fix, in addition to the likely path of the Mariner / Illustration by Meilan Solly


Lieutenant Robert F. Cox, the pilot of an unrelated flight coming out of the same air station, picked up that first concerning transmission shortly before 3:45 p.m., according to [declassified records](https://catalog.archives.gov/id/146850136?objectPage=342) digitized by the National Archives. In a later interview with investigators, Cox recounted establishing contact with Taylor, who informed him that “both my compasses are out. … I am over land, but it’s broken. I’m sure I’m in the \[Florida\] Keys, but I don’t know how far down, and I don’t know how to get to Fort Lauderdale.”

Cox, who didn’t yet realize that [Flight 19](https://www.usni.org/magazines/naval-history-magazine/2021/october/mysterious-disappearance-flight-19) couldn’t have made it as far south as the Keys, advised Taylor to fly north, with the setting sun visible to the west on his left wing. He also offered to fly toward the lost pilot’s location to pick the men up. Taylor waved Cox off, reassuring his fellow lieutenant that he now knew where he was. “Don’t come after me,” Taylor said—a statement that would later assume mythical significance in the [supernatural theories](https://www.wnyc.org/story/charles-berlitz/) that proliferated after Flight 19 vanished.

Lieutenant Charles Carroll Taylor


Courtesy of Naval Air Station Fort Lauderdale Museum

But Cox noticed something strange: The squadron’s transmissions were growing weaker. He realized that Taylor’s formation couldn’t be flying north from the Keys, which would have brought the group closer to his own position off Fort Lauderdale, on Florida’s east coast. Instead, the five [Grumman TBF Avengers](https://airandspace.si.edu/air-and-space-quarterly/spring-2022/avenger) seemed to be straying farther from the coastline, toward the Bahamas and the vast expanse of the Atlantic Ocean.

A flurry of confused communications ensued among ground units and those trying to advise Flight 19. Around 4:25 p.m., Taylor asked if anyone in the area had a radar screen that could pick up the group’s location in real time, but no such [technology](https://timeandnavigation.si.edu/navigating-air/challenges/overcoming-challenges/radio-navigation) was readily available. An air-sea task unit based in Port Everglades, a seaport in Fort Lauderdale, [alerted pilots](https://www.history.navy.mil/content/history/nhhc/about-us/leadership/director/directors-corner/h-grams/h-gram-057/h-057-4.html) and potential rescuers along the Florida coast of the squadron’s situation, simultaneously asking about available navigation equipment and [requesting that](https://archive.org/details/disappearanceoff00kusc/page/26/mode/1up?q=%22for+the+planes+to+change+to%22) Taylor switch to a less-trafficked emergency radio frequency.

The sheer volume of radio communications makes it difficult to know how much of the back and forth actually got through to Flight 19, but subsequent [accident reports](https://www.history.navy.mil/research/library/online-reading-room/title-list-alphabetically/l/lost-fight-19-official-accident-reports.html) highlighted efforts to advise Taylor and his men to fly west, “into the sun.” “If these directions had been heard and carried out,” investigators later concluded, “we are certain this flight would have returned to base safely.”

Around 4:45 p.m., Taylor relayed that the group planned to “fly north to make sure we are not over the Gulf of Mexico,” off Florida’s west coast. Half an hour later, he seemed to concur with outside observers’ advice, stating that the men would fly west “until we hit the beach or run out of gas.” Taylor reminded the other Avengers to remain in formation and, if gas got low, to ditch together.

At 6:04 p.m., Taylor made a crucial decision—one that likely doomed Flight 19. He concluded that they “didn’t go far enough east—turn around again—we may just as well turn around and go east again.” As lights were being lit at air stations and on ships up and down the Florida coast in hopes of guiding the men home, they were unknowingly flying in the opposite direction.

When ground units were finally able to calculate a [navigational fix](https://www.starpath.com/cgi-bin/web_card/courses/glossary.pl?show_def=2118&cat=Marine_Radar) (an estimate of the squadron’s coordinates) around 6 p.m., it was estimated to be within 100 miles of 29 degrees north latitude, 79 degrees west longitude— [well north](https://www.google.com/maps/place/29%C2%B000'00.0%22N+79%C2%B000'00.0%22W/@25.8299924,-82.7775085,6.73z/data=!4m4!3m3!8m2!3d29!4d-79?entry=ttu&g_ep=EgoyMDI1MTExNy4wIKXMDSoASAFQAw%3D%3D) of the Bahamas and roughly east of Daytona Beach, Florida.

In retrospect, it is an agonizing irony that by this point, rescue units could no longer reach Flight 19 directly, but they could still hear garbled transmissions between the five planes. The [last known message](https://archive.org/details/disappearanceoff00kusc/page/44/mode/1up?q=%22ensign+bossi%27s+faint+calls%22) came from Ensign [Joseph Bossi](https://www.nasflmuseum.com/bossi.html), one of the planes’ pilots, who tried to reach Taylor at 7:04 p.m. No response was heard.

## What happened to Flight 19?

Exactly what happened next remains a mystery. Did the men stay together, run out of fuel and successfully ditch their planes? If so, how long did they survive in the water? Taylor had been involved in three [previous ditches](https://www.sun-sentinel.com/1995/12/03/50-years-after-the-lost-patrol-vanishing-of-27-still-a-mystery/), and the Avengers were almost certainly equipped with life rafts.

The Navy’s Board of Investigation later [concluded](https://catalog.archives.gov/id/146850136?objectPage=305) “that the state of the sea in the area in which Flight 19 was presumed to have landed was rough and unfavorable for a water landing.”

The most likely outcome is that the men “ran out of gas and went into the ocean,” says John Bloom, director of the [Naval Air Station Fort Lauderdale Museum](https://www.nasflmuseum.com/), which hosts an annual [memorial ceremony](https://www.nasflmuseum.com/memorial.html) on the anniversary of the disappearance. But any certainty of what transpired after all communications ended was lost along with [the men](https://www.nasflmuseum.com/flight-19-exhibit.html) who died on Flight 19.

Those killed included five pilots and nine crew members and trainees, some of them still teenagers, yet [also](https://www.nasflmuseum.com/devlin.html) [veterans](https://www.nasflmuseum.com/gruebel.html) of World War II, which had ended just [three months earlier](https://www.nationalww2museum.org/war/topics/end-world-war-ii-1945). Taylor had more than 2,500 hours of flying experience, 61 of them in combat operations. [Edward Joseph Powers Jr.](https://www.nasflmuseum.com/powers.html), a 26-year-old Marine captain, left behind a wife and child.

The tragedy of losing these 14 men was soon compounded after a fellow Navy pilot, Lieutenant [Walter G. Jeffrey](https://www.findagrave.com/memorial/13717545/walter_george_'walt'-jeffery), volunteered to fly one of two [Martin PBM-5 Mariners](https://airandspace.si.edu/collection-objects/martin-pbm-5a-mariner/nasm_A19730270000) that departed from NAS [Banana River](https://ccspacemuseum.org/artifacts/banana-river-naval-air-station/), around 150 miles north of the Fort Lauderdale station, at 7:27 p.m., in the direction of the Avengers’ last presumed location.

Within half an hour, the Mariner suddenly fell off radio contact. Later that night, a ship in the vicinity reported seeing a “ [burst of flames](https://web.archive.org/web/20120127041154/http:/www.history.navy.mil/nan/backissues/1970s/1973/jun73.pdf)” estimated to be 100 feet tall around 7:50 p.m. By the time searchers reached the site, no trace of the Mariner or its 13-man crew could be found. (Mariners were sometimes dubbed “ [flying gas tanks](https://warhistory.org/@msw/article/martin-pbm-mariner)” due to their high fuel capacity and propensity to experience onboard fires.)

With yet another plane missing, the search took on an additional layer of complexity. It lasted five days, with more than 200 planes scouring the Atlantic each day in hopes of finding the 27 men. Sightings of flares and flashes, of life jackets and rafts, fueled hope, even if they turned out to be imagined or unrelated debris. The cycle of hope and disappointment made headlines: On December 9, for example, the [United Press](https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-billings-gazette-plane-searchers-sig/185387682/) news agency reported the discovery of six rafts, but officials [soon retracted](https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-herald-sun-mystery-of-27-missing-air/185387785/) the sighting as a mistake. The [Associated Press](https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-herald-statesman-widest-search-ever/185387843/) deemed the rescue mission “the most extensive search ever undertaken along the Atlantic Seaboard.”

## A mother’s fight to clear her son’s name

When Taylor’s mother, [Katherine Taylor](https://www.caller.com/story/news/special-reports/building-our-future/throwback/2023/01/12/how-a-corpus-christi-family-was-tied-to-lore-of-the-bermuda-triangle/69796696007/), received a telegram informing her that her son was missing, she was at the front of her classroom in Corpus Christi, the Texas city where she’d raised him. She was so devastated by the news that she walked out, never to return.

One of the central questions asked by both Katherine and the Navy was how Taylor, a seasoned pilot with extensive flying experience, could become so disoriented that he believed he was over the Keys. Within days, the Navy convened an investigation board, which focused on the testimony of those involved in the search along the coast.

Investigators’ [initial report](https://www.ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USN/rep/Flight19/index.html), issued internally in January 1946, acknowledged that elements of the search protocol could have been improved, including coordination between units and rescuers’ ability to get a bearing on the flights. But it heavily weighted Taylor’s disorientation, noting that he wasn’t sure where the Florida peninsula was at the time, and that “this uncertainty influenced his later decisions.”

Bloom points out that the area was unfamiliar terrain for Taylor, who had transferred up from Miami the previous month. This may explain the pilot’s belief that he was over the Keys instead of the Bahamas. The squadron “never said another word about their compass not working,” Bloom says. “But then they did fly north thinking they’d see the peninsula, and when they didn’t, they thought they were out over the Gulf, so \[Taylor\] flies east.” What would have been obvious to aviators who had been based at the air station longer—that it would have been impossible for the group to be over the Keys so soon after taking off—seemed to be less apparent to the members of Flight 19 in their duress and confusion.

The Navy was less magnanimous in its assessment of Taylor. “The leader of the flight became so hopelessly confused as to have suffered something akin to a mental aberration,” the chief of naval air training wrote.

That conclusion did not sit well with Katherine. She believed there were holes in the Navy’s official version, which relied heavily on rescuers’ testimony in the absence of physical evidence and the aviators’ own accounts. As Katherine wrote in a letter to the mother of [Walter Reed Parpart Jr.](https://www.nasflmuseum.com/walter-parpart.html), the 18-year-old radioman with Taylor on his plane, she didn’t believe the planes had been properly instructed to come west, or that their approximate location was ascertained and communicated to searchers in a timely fashion.

“It just can’t be possible that so many could disappear without a trace,” Katherine [told a reporter](https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-miami-news/148641210/) in March 1946, after taking a trip to Florida to interview those involved in the rescue effort. “I shall never give up until I find out why!” She proved to be a tenacious advocate for this viewpoint, and officials concluded that she could not be swayed. “It is believed that Mrs. Taylor is emotionally unstable as a result of this disaster,” the Navy noted in its report.

One of Katherine’s key contentions centered around the ready plane, a standby aircraft that could have been sent up quickly from NAS Fort Lauderdale. In his testimony, Cox—the pilot who’d first realized Flight 19 was lost—testified that he’d returned to the station and asked Lieutenant Commander Donald J. Poole, the officer in charge of training flights there, if he could take the ready plane and fly northeast to the squadron’s likely position. Cox recalled that Poole “very definitely said no, that he didn’t think there was any use in sending it out then.”

In his own testimony, Poole outlined several reasons for his response, including waiting on the fix and fear that sending an additional plane would “complicate” communications from the ground. None of these explanations was particularly convincing to Katherine, nor to Cox himself.

“My dad felt extremely strongly that he could locate the flight if he were allowed to go back up, and he was very frustrated that he wasn’t allowed to,” Cox’s daughter, Colby Cox, said in a 2021 [History Channel documentary](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qYmY4g3MDOI).

Bloom, for his part, says, “They should have been proactively doing something. … The plane was up and ready.”

Poole’s testimony provides some insight into the prevailing mindset about the situation’s severity. “I was still confident and certain that they would hit the coast before their gas supply ran out,” he told investigators. Lieutenant Commander Charles Kenyon, operations officer at NAS Fort Lauderdale, similarly testified that he “figured they were temporarily confused and … they would come back right on time.”

In 1947, after tireless lobbying by Katherine, the Navy modified its findings away from the initial focus on Taylor. A corrections review board concluded “that the flight disappeared for reasons or causes unknown.” This would stand as the official word on Flight 19 in the decades that followed.

As the Navy closed the book on the case, a plethora of conspiracy theories emerged to fill the information void.

## Flight 19 conspiracy theories and the Bermuda Triangle

In 1950, the [Associated Press](https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-miami-news-atlantic-is-still-deep-my/185404139/) published an article on plane and ship disappearances off the U.S.’s southeastern coast, including Flight 19, which it erroneously suggested had disappeared under complete radio silence. “It is the same big world the ancients knew into which men and their machines and ships can disappear without a trace,” the AP noted ominously.

Fourteen years later, in 1964, the men’s magazine [_Argosy_](https://www.pulpmags.org/content/info/argosy.html) published an article by Vincent Gaddis titled “ [The Deadly Bermuda Triangle](https://www.physics.smu.edu/pseudo/BermudaTriangle/vincentgaddis.txt).” The piece was the first to popularize this term, which refers to a roughly 500,000- to 1.5-million-square-mile area of the North Atlantic Ocean, stretching from Florida’s east coast to Bermuda to the Greater Antilles. But the area Gaddis described had already developed a [fearsome reputation](https://archive.nytimes.com/www.nytimes.com/fodors/top/features/travel/destinations/bermudaandcaribbean/bermuda/fdrs_feat_29_8.html?n=Top/Features/Travel/Destinations/Bermuda) among mariners of centuries past, who navigated a sometimes stormy and hurricane-prone section of the ocean, fueled by the warm currents of the Gulf Stream, without the benefit of accurate weather forecasting.

Did you know? _The Tempest_ and the Bermuda Triangle

- Some scholars [posit](https://www.shakespeare-online.com/keydates/tempestbermuda.html) that [William Shakespeare](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/the-real-history-behind-hamnet-and-the-tragically-short-life-of-william-shakespeare-and-anne-hathaways-only-son-180987733/) based his play [_The Tempest_](https://www.folger.edu/explore/shakespeares-works/the-tempest/read/) on accounts of the [_Sea Venture_](https://encyclopediavirginia.org/entries/sea-venture/), an English ship wrecked off Bermuda in 1609.
- Others disagree, suggesting the Bard was inspired by a [near-shipwreck](https://www.rsc.org.uk/news/archive/the-ship-that-inspired-shakespeare) closer to home, or that he simply [drew on](https://shakespeareoxfordfellowship.org/bermuda-shipwreck-of-1609/) his imagination.

As the Bermuda Triangle descriptor took hold in the popular imagination, it was applied retroactively to events that had taken place in the same radius, such as the [disappearance of the USS _Cyclops_](https://www.history.com/articles/bermuda-triangle-uss-cyclops-mystery-world-war-i), a World War I transport ship that vanished in 1918 with its crew of 306 after transmitting a [final message](https://www.warhistoryonline.com/war-articles/tragedy-at-sea-still-a-mystery.html): “Weather fair. All well.”

“Whatever this menace that lurks within a triangle of tragedy so close to home,” Gaddis wrote, “it was responsible for the most incredible mystery in the history of aviation—the lost patrol.” (Despite the staying power of this nickname for Flight 19, the group was a squadron on a training run, not a patrol.) In his best-selling 1974 book, _The Bermuda Triangle_, [Charles Berlitz](https://www.sfgate.com/bayarea/article/Charles-Berlitz-Bermuda-Triangle-author-2832395.php), a chronicler of paranormal phenomena, [deemed](https://archive.org/details/bermudatriangle0000char/page/21/mode/1up?q=%22first+occasion%22) the loss of the Avengers and the Mariner “the first occasion in which planes were affected,” building on his long—and [largely unreliable](https://skepdic.com/bermuda.html)—list of ships that had vanished in the area.

The fact that the Mariner disappeared in approximately the same vicinity as Flight 19 fueled theories of supernatural intervention, with outside observers ignoring the fact that a rescue plane would naturally be sent to its target’s presumed location.

Berlitz also cited a request by Taylor to be dismissed from the assignment ahead of time as a “presentiment of disaster.” But those who actually saw Taylor on the day of the training run didn’t notice anything amiss, and the lieutenant never officially escalated his request. Berlitz also falsely relayed Taylor’s message of “don’t come after me” as “don’t come after me … they look like they are from outer space.” That line really clinched the legend of the Bermuda Triangle: “This final mystery, with its suggestion of other-world interference, is echoed in more than a few of the other disappearances,” Berlitz wrote.

Bloom, for his part, dismisses such speculation. “I don’t believe in fiction,” he says. “There’s a lot of misstatements about what was said when they were out there.”

The 1977 Spielberg film [_Close Encounters of the Third Kind_](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt0075860/) [depicted](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gkBIToB43g4&t=62s) the crew of Flight 19 re-emerging years later, without aging, after being abducted by aliens. Musicians have tackled the legend of the Bermuda Triangle, too: [Fleetwood Mac](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f83nXE0TBvA) sang of “all of these ships and planes, a great big mystery that cannot be explained,” while [Barry Manilow](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=G9BCWO2KHHI) mused on a place that “makes people disappear.” Numerous [television dramas](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt0452573/) and [documentaries](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=plalHGHwSj8) have sustained this block of ocean’s notoriety well into contemporary times.

In the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration’s [view](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/bermudatri.html), “There is no evidence that mysterious disappearances occur with any greater frequency in the Bermuda Triangle than in any other large, well-traveled area of the ocean.” The myth is fueled by fanciful interpretations of the many unknowns of such disappearances, which can often be attributed to environmental factors like tropical storms and hurricanes, sudden changes in weather, and shallow waters in the Caribbean Sea.

The real—and perhaps most unsolvable—mysteries of Flight 19 are what Taylor experienced that day that led him off course in the first place, and what happened after the last radio call from Bossi to Taylor at 7:04 p.m. Though the emergence of conspiracy theories has obscured this, many [aviation](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/the-enduring-mystery-of-a-plane-that-vanished-in-the-icy-canadian-wilderness-with-44-people-on-board-180985878/) and naval tragedies have resulted from a confluence of banal mishaps and missed opportunities. What might have happened if the ready plane flew sooner? Or if the location fix had come in earlier and been heard by Flight 19?

For all the fabrications and exaggerations that Gaddis detailed in his 1964 magazine article, one statement rings all too true today: “The sea guards well her secrets.”
